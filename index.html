I

A Recantation at the Horizon

A year ago I wrote a foreword for this volume and called it Sentient Horizon. Its first sentence declared that “perhaps we have reached a point where governance itself is learning to feel.” I stand by the instinct. I recant the geography. A horizon is something you look towards: distant, luminous, always receding, flattering to the one who gazes. Donald Innes Davies has spent the intervening year rewriting this volume from the ground up — and it is the ground he has found, not the horizon. The book that now carries the title Reconstituting the Responsible Organisation: Form, Relationship and the Practice of Organising does not look up. It looks down, beneath the floorboards of the formal organisation, to the root-work that actually holds the house together.

So this foreword must be radically remade, because the book it introduces has been. Edition One spoke of advanced governance, of innovation, of corporate agility and anticipatory coherence. Those words are not abandoned — Donald is too careful an archivist of his own thought for that — but each has been taken down to the workshop, dismantled, examined against the grain, and rebuilt to bear weight. Agility is no longer speed; it is capacity, and capacity has a cost someone carries. Anticipatory coherence is no longer a gift of the sensitive leader; it is a set of provisions — time, cover, a route, a return date — without which foresight is merely mood. Symbiotic governance is no longer a harmony; it is a ledger of dependence, reciprocity and asymmetry that must be read line by line.

Governance, I now think, is not learning to feel. It is learning to notice where it is already being felt — at ten past six in the evening, in a private message to a colleague whose shift has ended, on a shelf in a workshop where a removed assembly waits with a handwritten label. Governance is learning to feel the floor.

NOT THE HORIZON — THE GROUND.NOT THE DIAL — THE ROOT.

What follows is an attempt to read the whole of Donald’s volume through one demanding lens, and to let the volume answer back. The lens is this: that the leverage point — that most seductive concept of systems thinking — must be re-seen from inside a Deleuzian assemblage, not as a knob to be turned from outside, but as a rhizome: an immanent, interconnected entryway through which the whole arrangement of relations can shift. I will argue that Volume Four is, whether or not it uses the vocabulary, the most thorough field manual of rhizomatic leverage yet written for practitioners of governance. And I will argue that it supplies something Deleuze and Guattari themselves never fully supplied: a conscience for the line of flight.

A second instrument travels with the first, and this foreword will keep it in hand throughout. If the rhizome tells us where leverage lives — in the middle, in the fissure, below the floor — it does not tell us how to move once we are there. For that we need a discipline of steering: what I shall call, following Dee Hock and the practitioners who took up his coinage, chaordic navigation. It is the art of holding a course along the narrow, living edge where order and chaos interpenetrate — neither clamping the rhizome into a tree nor surrendering it to the runaway spread that devours its own ground. I will argue that Donald, who names the chaordic condition in his Glossary and keeps the Greek kubernáo — to steer — at the root of governance, is one of the most exacting chaordic navigators now writing, and that his leverage points are best understood not as places to push but as bearings by which an organisation steers through the edge.

THE RHIZOME SHOWS WHERE.THE CHAORD SHOWS HOW TO STEER.
II

The Fortnight That Would Not End

Every great book has a primal scene. Volume Four’s is a page and a half long, and it is placed — with exquisite modesty — before the Introduction, under the heading A Threshold Before We Begin. “The arrangement was meant to last a fortnight.” A colleague is absent; a decision cannot wait; those closest to the work improvise. Someone checks the unfamiliar cases. Someone tells the next team what has changed. Several months later the arrangement is still in use. Its formal description has scarcely changed. At a review, someone asks whether the temporary arrangement should now be authorised — and the question, which appeared straightforward, detonates. One person describes a quicker route; another the extra checking that made it dependable; a third has begun relying on a colleague whose responsibilities never included that work; a newcomer believes it is simply the procedure.

“The same practice carried different meanings and different burdens.” Then, in three lines that I regard as the volume’s whole philosophy in miniature: “The review was already participating in the change. Questions altered what people noticed. Explanations changed what others expected.”

Here is the rhizome in its native habitat. Nothing was designed. No dial was turned. A small accommodation sent runners underground, and by the time anyone came to authorise it, it had become structural — load-bearing, peopled, freighted with expectation. The formal chart above ground remained unchanged while, beneath it, the organisation had already become something else. This is precisely what Deleuze and Guattari meant when they set the rhizome against the tree: the tree has a root, a trunk, a hierarchy of branches; the rhizome has none, and “any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and must be.”

Donald does not reach for that vocabulary. He reaches for transformative teleology — the account, drawn from Ralph Stacey, Douglas Griffin and Patricia Shaw, in which people act intentionally while their interaction transforms the very purposes they pursue. The two languages converge on a single, unsettling truth: the organisation is always being remade in the middle, by people who think they are only keeping the work going.

The book as assemblage

The form of the volume enacts its argument. It is built of eight Movements — the musical term is deliberate — and each Movement is itself a heterogeneous arrangement: an Intellectual Lineage and Continuing Conversation that convenes a chorus of thinkers without forcing them to agree; six analytical Sections; the terse, italic-breathed fragments called The Margin; a Crucible of Practice that drops the reader into a single situation; an Interlude of near-silence; an Orientation for Practice; numbered Signals (forty-six across the volume); and a Coda. Around them stand a Glossary and a Bibliography of well over a hundred and fifty works.

This is not a treatise with a trunk. It is a plateau-structure in the strict Deleuzian sense: regions of sustained intensity that can be entered from anywhere and that connect laterally. A manager can open the book at Signal 12 — “The private relationship carrying public responsibility” — and be pulled sideways into Bourdieu, back into the Crucible of Movement Three, forward into Iris Marion Young. The Margins, especially, behave like rhizomatic nodes: tiny, dense, connective, refusing hierarchy. “The meeting ends on time. The work it has set in motion does not.” One could build a governance practice from the Margins alone.

Two architectures hold the plateaus in relation. The first, the Architecture of Responsible Organisations, is Donald’s synthesis of purpose, authority, knowledge, relationship and practical provision — the question of how people become able to carry responsibility and obtain a response capable of changing what happens. The second, the Invisible Architecture, gathers eight orientations — Meaning, Order, Imagination, Governance, Memory, Transformation, Participation, Continuance — through which formal arrangements acquire lived significance. The first is the stratum. The second is the soil beneath it. Leverage, as I shall argue, lives in the relation between them.

III

Against the Knob

Donella Meadows gave the systems tradition its most quoted definition: leverage points are “places within a complex system … where a small shift in one thing can produce big changes in everything.” Her ladder of twelve such places — from constants and buffers at the bottom to goals, paradigms and “the power to transcend paradigms” at the top — remains one of the great gifts of the discipline, and Donald honours the tradition by placing Thinking in Systems in his bibliography. Meadows herself warned that leverage points are counter-intuitive, that people find them and then push them in the wrong direction.

Yet the ladder has been read, in a thousand consultancy decks, as a control panel. The leverage point becomes a dial: external to the system, locatable on a map, operable by the one who holds the map. Pull here, get that. It is the cybernetics of the thermostat applied to human beings — and it carries a buried politics, because the one who turns the dial is always presumed to stand outside what is turned.

Volume Four dismantles the dial from within, and it does so with evidence rather than polemic. Consider how often its Crucibles stage the success of a lever as the beginning of the trouble. In Movement One a weekly report shows fewer customers waiting; the manager pronounces the team “more agile” and proposes rolling the approach out to two more teams. Signal 5 names what has happened: “The Measure Has Become the Whole Story.” In Movement Four, a completion figure reaches nearly one hundred per cent while a technician keeps returning to a machine whose intermittent sound the standard check cannot reproduce. Norbert Wiener’s 1960 warning — that a mechanism may faithfully achieve the purpose specified in it while betraying the purpose intended — is brought down from automation to the inspection form: “The count then answers a narrower question than the one for which it is being used.”

The dial, in other words, does not fail because it is badly calibrated. It fails because it is outside. It touches a representation of the system and mistakes that touch for contact with the system itself. Donald’s whole method — tracing cases through their passage, asking who waited, who stayed late, what was displaced — is a refusal of exteriority. He goes in.

IV

The Assemblage Named

An assemblage — agencement, in the French that loses something in every translation — is a provisional grouping of heterogeneous elements: bodies, tools, words, rules, desires, buildings, technologies, that hold together for a time and could come apart. Deleuze and Guattari give it two axes. On one, a machinic assemblage of bodies and materials meets a collective assemblage of enunciation — words, categories, statements. On the other, movements of territorialisation that stabilise and give identity meet movements of deterritorialisation that carry the arrangement away from itself. Manuel DeLanda later made the decisive point for social theory: the parts of an assemblage are related by exteriority — they can be detached and plugged into other assemblages — so the whole is never a sealed organism.

Now look again at Donald’s Crucibles. Each is an assemblage of exactly this kind, rendered with novelistic precision. In Movement One: a telephone, a scheduler who knows the diary, a technical colleague, a shared queue, a weekly report, a customer’s premises, and the hour of six o’clock. In Movement Two: a decision paper, a spreadsheet, a departing manager, a successor, a forty-minute case recorded as one answer within target, and an unfinished sentence. In Movement Three: an authority matrix, a late shift, a duty manager, private messages, an appendix, and a spare chair. In Movement Five: a supplier’s diagram comparing a component to a natural form, a discount deadline, a returns credit, a workshop shelf. In Movement Seven: a portal, a minimum purchase commitment, one unreserved assembly and two requests. In Movement Eight: a schedule cleared of appointments, and a doorway through which the requests that were never appointments keep arriving.

These are not “cases” in the business-school sense, where a clean problem awaits a clever solution. They are arrangements of relations in which material elements (stock, rotas, rooms, equipment) and expressive elements (a category, a phrase, a label, a minute) are perpetually catching on one another. And in every one, the moment of real leverage arrives at a junction between the two — where a word meets a body, where a record meets a shift.

THE LEVER IS NEVER WHERE THE CHART SAYS IT IS.
V

Chaordic Navigation: Steering at the Edge

The word arrived at a dinner. In March 1993, speaking at the Santa Fe Institute — then the gathering-place of the new sciences of complexity — Dee Hock offered a neologism compounded of chaos and order. A chaord, as he later defined it in Birth of the Chaordic Age (1999), is any self-organising, self-governing, adaptive, nonlinear, complex organism, organisation, community or system, “the behavior of which harmoniously blends characteristics of both chaos and order.” Hock was not a theorist who found an example. He was a practitioner who found a word for what he had already built.

What he had built was VISA. In the late 1960s the BankAmericard licensing system was in disarray — inconsistent contracts, no standardisation, no dispute resolution, mounting fraud. Hock, then at a licensee bank in Seattle, proposed that the problem be studied systematically and was made chair of the committee for his trouble. Out of that work came, in 1970, National BankAmericard Inc., renamed VISA in 1976: an organisation owned by its member banks, in which hundreds of fiercely competing institutions agreed to a common purpose and common principles, including the principles by which those principles themselves might evolve. Power and function were distributed; the centre could not compel its members to adopt a product; innovation arose at the edges and travelled when it proved itself. Fierce competition and deep cooperation were made to occupy the same body. That, for Hock, was the chaord in practice.

Purpose and principles as the genetic code

Hock’s design discipline has six elements — purpose, principles, people, concept, structure, practice — and he insisted that they are not a sequence but a set of lenses, each feeding back into the others. Purpose is “a clear, simple statement of intent that identifies and binds the community together.” A principle is “a precept against which all structures, decisions, actions, and results will be judged”; principles describe rather than prescribe. A concept is the perception of a structure “that all may trust to be equitable, just, and effective.” Only then come structure and practice. Hock called purpose and principles the genetic code of an institution: not a rulebook, but the deep pattern from which a thousand local decisions can self-organise without a central hand. Hence his most famous aphorism, which stands at the head of this foreword: simple, clear purpose and principles give rise to complex, intelligent behaviour; complex rules and regulations give rise to simple, stupid behaviour.

And beneath the design, an ethic. “Where behaviour is compelled, there lies tyranny, however petty.” Leadership, for Hock, educes rather than induces — it draws out what participants are willing to bring. He warned that the hardest work is not getting new ideas into the mind but clearing out the old: “Every mind is a room filled with archaic furniture.”

The edge, the path and its two ditches

Hock’s coinage fell into fertile ground. Christopher Langton had located computation and life at a phase transition he called “the edge of chaos”; Stuart Kauffman — whom Donald convenes in Movement Five — argued that self-organisation, not selection alone, builds biological order, and that adaptive systems poise themselves near that edge. In organisation theory, Ralph Stacey’s The Chaos Frontier (1991) and Managing Chaos (1992) — the latter cited by Donald as the root of Edition One’s strategic agility — described the creative zone of bounded instability, poised between stable equilibrium and explosive disintegration. Pascale, Millemann and Gioja would later popularise the phrase surfing the edge of chaos.

The practitioners of the Art of Hosting — Toke Paludan Møller, Monica Nissén, Chris Corrigan and their many colleagues — turned Hock’s idea into a working map they call the chaordic path. On one side lies chaos; past it, a ditch of destructive disorder they call chaos without order — “chamos.” On the other side lies order; past it, the ditch of stifling control. Between them runs a narrow path where form emerges and collective intelligence can do its work. Leadership, on this map, is the work of the chaos–order edge, where current ways cannot meet a new challenge; management is the work of the order–control edge, where routine must reliably hold a standard. Both are needed. The failure is to fall into either ditch. Corrigan’s chaordic stepping stones — need, purpose, principles, participants, concept, limiting beliefs, structure, practice — give the path its practical rhythm, and he describes them as the basis of all his facilitative work.

Navigation, not control

Why navigation? Because the oldest name for governance is a sailor’s word. Donald’s own Glossary reminds us that kubernáo — to steer — stands at the linguistic root of both cybernetics and governance, and glosses it with precision: “acting with direction while attending to changing conditions and consequences.” The helmsman does not command the sea. She holds a bearing, reads the water, tacks against the wind, and arrives by a course no chart could have drawn in advance. Edwin Hutchins, whose Cognition in the Wild Donald draws into Movement Seven, studied exactly this: the navigation of a ship as a cognitive accomplishment distributed across people, instruments and representations, belonging to no single mind on the bridge.

Deleuze and Guattari, too, chose the sea. In their treatise on smooth and striated space, the sea is the smooth space par excellence — open, directional, crossed by trajectories rather than enclosed by property lines — and also the first space to be progressively striated by the instruments of navigation: star-fixes, longitude, the grid. Smooth and striated, they insist, never exist in pure form; each is continually translated into the other. That mixture is precisely the chaord. And it gives us the formula on which the rest of this foreword depends: the rhizome is the smooth space of the organisation; chaordic navigation is the art of crossing it with just enough striation to arrive — and no more.

NOT COMMAND — COURSE.NOT CONTROL — BEARING.

How Donald steers: the chaordic grammar of leverage

Donald rarely uses the word chaordic in the body of this volume; he places it in the Glossary as the chaordic condition — “the relationship between order and chaos” that directs attention to “shared purpose, coherence and arrangements that allow initiative and self-organisation to develop through participation.” But the condition saturates the book. Every Movement refuses both ditches. Formal authority is defended against romantic informality — “a customer should not have to depend on knowing the most influential member of staff” — and informal coalitions are defended against bureaucratic suspicion. “Organisational evolution cannot therefore be reduced to a predictable sequence from informal creativity to mature bureaucracy.” “A culture of constant change can erode the very capacity it seeks to improve.” The path is narrow and Donald walks it on every page.

More precisely, his leverage points are navigational instruments. Read the Crucibles, Orientations and Signals for their method, and seven instruments recur:

First, the enabling constraint. A six-week trial with stated limits, defined participants and a power to stop it early: “enabling constraints: they give the experiment a responsible form without specifying everything it must discover.” This is Hock’s principle-over-rule in practical dress — a boundary that releases rather than confines.

Second, the return date. Chrono-governance gives every provisional decision a moment when its consequences must come back for judgement — “the interval between a decision and its reconsideration is part of governance design.” Too long, and the ship holds a failing course; too short, and it never learns what its course is doing. The review date is the navigator’s tack.

Third, the explicit boundary of discretion. Which decisions can be made locally, which require consultation while local ownership remains, which pass to specialist authority — the triage at the heart of Movement Three. A bounded delegation is a striation laid lightly on smooth space: enough to coordinate, not so much that local judgement is starved.

Fourth, governance as groundwork — reinforcing conditions where ambiguity leaves someone exposed, loosening them where an inherited boundary prevents judgement, withdrawing them where a requirement no longer protects. These are the three moves of the chaordic navigator: trim, ease, release.

Fifth, the signal as sounding. The forty-six Signals are not indicators to be maximised; they are depth-soundings, telling the navigator when the keel is nearing a ditch. “The Measure Has Become the Whole Story” warns of the control ditch; “An invitation acquires a wider purpose” warns of the chaos ditch.

Sixth, the statement of what is in force now. Again and again, Donald insists that in the interval of uncertainty someone must say what remains authorised — “continuation pending review,” “the current route remains authorised until a further decision is communicated.” Without that fix, people steer by rumour, and authority “arrives after expectation.”

Seventh, the record that preserves uncertainty. A decision paper that names the alternatives, the open questions and the people who must be heard is a ship’s log: it lets the next navigator understand why the course was set, and when it may responsibly be changed.

Set beside Corrigan’s stepping stones, the correspondence is almost exact — with one decisive difference that I shall come to.

Where the chaord fails: Hock’s own warning

Chaordic navigation has a cautionary tale, and its author told it himself. Hock later acknowledged that he had underestimated how hard it would be to abandon old habits, and that VISA lacked what he called an institutional immune system to protect its principles. In 2008 the member-owned chaord became Visa Inc., a publicly traded corporation, its orientation shifting towards shareholder returns. The genetic code had been rewritten. A deterritorialised arrangement had been reterritorialised by capital — exactly as Deleuze and Guattari would have predicted, and exactly as Donald’s Crucibles keep showing at the scale of a team: the line of flight is recaptured unless it is provisioned and protected.

Here lies the difference between Donald and much chaordic literature. The chaordic path can be sung as a hymn to emergence: trust the edge, hold the space, the collective will find its way. Donald will not sing it. His navigator carries a ledger as well as a compass. Before he widens any fissure he asks who will provide the time, who will cover the absence, who can refuse, and where a concern goes when it exceeds local authority. The chaordic stepping stones begin with need and purpose; Donald’s begin there too, and then add a stone that the path rarely names — provision — and another that it names too softly — answerability. A purpose that is clear and simple, he would say, still gives rise to simple, stupid behaviour if no one has been given the hours to pursue it. That is the institutional immune system Hock found missing. Volume Four is, in large part, a manual for growing one.

A COMPASS WITHOUT A LEDGER STEERS INTO SOMEONE ELSE’S STORM.
VI

Eight Plateaus, Eight Fissures

What follows is a passage through the eight Movements, asking of each a single rhizomatic question: where is the fissure — the microscopic crack through which the arrangement can be reorganised from within — and what does Donald insist we see before we widen it?

Movement One · The Capacity to Adapt — the call before the question

The Movement relocates agility from velocity to capacity: “the capacity to notice consequential change, develop a sufficiently informed response and revise that response in the light of experience.” Its lineage is broad — de Geus’s living company, Senge’s disciplines, Beer’s Viable System Model and Hoverstadt’s fractal design, Arthur and Holland on adaptive processes, Hurst and Miller on strength turning to constraint, Weick, Schön, Argyris, Edmondson, Schein, Rodgers, Follett, Freeman, Bauman, Raworth, Habermas, and Harari read as a question rather than a prophecy. The sentence on which everything turns is spoken by an experienced service colleague: “It is mostly the same process. We speak before we send the question.”

That is a rhizome described in eight words. Not a new structure: a new connection, lateral, underground, between scheduling, technical support and service — functions the formal route kept in separate rooms. Its vibrations travel at once: fewer waiting customers, a manager’s pride, a request to extend, another manager’s suggestion that faster confirmation should allow more bookings with the same staff. And then the reterritorialisation, perfectly observed: “Can we start sending our difficult bookings to you?” The line of flight is captured; the colleague who opened the passage becomes, in the eyes of others, the route. Donald’s lesson is the first law of rhizomatic leverage: a fissure that is not given provision will be colonised by the very demand it relieved.

Chaordic bearing. Movement One is Donald’s lesson in the first tack. The manager’s instinct — “Then we can add that to the guidance” — is a lurch towards the control ditch, codifying a conversation into a rule; the neighbouring team’s request to route its difficult bookings through one colleague is a slide towards the chaos ditch. Donald steers between: keep the practice where it works, examine a few real cases including those answered after hours, defer wider adoption, and promise no one evening cover. A bounded course, a stated limit, a date to look again.

Movement Two · The Time of Organising — the sentence left unfinished

Here chrono-governance, Edition One’s great coinage, is given what Donald calls “a closer account.” Fayol, Weber and Barnard meet Wiener, Forrester and Beer; Weick and Schön meet Gunderson and Holling’s panarchy; Prigogine’s irreversibility is admitted only “in the limited analogical sense”; Naomi Klein is summoned to remind us that crisis concentrates authority. The clocks of a single decision — operational, managerial, governance, relational — are shown running at different speeds through one agenda item.

The fissure in this Movement is the most beautiful in the book. An outgoing manager and a specialist sit with twenty minutes to spare. “It works provided…,” the specialist says — and stops. The manager writes beneath the assumption that support can be provided within existing capacity: “Checking time and cover to be examined at review.” The specialist changes “checking time” to “time to learn and check.” That is all. And because of that, the forum later allocates protected time, names cover, brings forward the review of displaced work and authorises the successor to narrow the boundary.

This is the expressive element injected into the material structure — precisely the mechanism by which, on the rhizomatic view, a phrase can “instantly link up” with the frustrated reality of the work and reorganise the arrangement. A few words in a handover note pluck the string; the whole apparatus vibrates. But Donald will not let us romanticise it. The phrase acquired force only because a manager made the alteration, a successor read it, and a forum had authority to respond. The fissure was microscopic; the provision that let it widen was not.

It is in this Movement, too, that Donald takes up the image I offered him in conversation in October 2025: the Key Maker, borrowed from The Matrix — one who recognises pathways through an architecture that others experience as separate rooms and closed doors. I am grateful, and more than grateful to be corrected. Donald turns the image back on itself: if every consequential boundary can be crossed only through one individual’s relationships, “the organisation has gained an opening and created a vulnerability.” “The pathway becomes more dependable when it can be used responsibly beyond its maker.” My metaphor has been made to become-other. Read rhizomatically, the Key Maker is not the keeper of keys at all; she is the one who finds the fissures — and the responsible organisation is the one that learns to cut keys of its own.

Chaordic bearing. Movement Two is chaordic navigation through time. The review date is the tack; the handover is the change of watch; the eight-week extension with protected time, named cover and authority to narrow the boundary is a corrected heading. Donald’s successor manager models the navigator’s hardest discipline — he does not pretend to a complete view, yet he states what is authorised now, because “his restraint could therefore create a different arrangement whose authority is less visible.” Steering includes saying where the ship is while the chart is still being drawn.

Movement Three · The Human Consequences of Organising — the spare chair

The most philosophically dense Movement brings form, authority and dignity together. Weber’s rational-legal office is defended as a protection — “a customer should not have to depend on knowing the most influential member of staff.” Locke’s entrusted authority, Nozick’s demand that every restriction justify itself, Habermas’s distinction between communicative and strategic action, and Arendt’s plurality, power and judgement are each applied with careful acknowledgement of their political scope. Bourdieu, Mintzberg, Foucault and Schein expose informal power; James C. Scott’s métis and Brown and Duguid’s social life of information defend practical knowledge; Sen, Nussbaum, Fraser, Piketty and Cornel West bring capability, recognition, distribution and moral criticism to bear.

The Crucible discovers that a delegation can exist on paper while the late shift receives specialist advice the next morning; that difficult cases have been travelling through private messages to an experienced colleague after hours; that the newer colleague’s concern became consequential only when a specialist recognised the knowledge it contained (Signal 13). And then, in the Interlude, the smallest leverage point in the entire volume: “There is a spare chair beside the desk… When the experienced colleague comes over, the newer colleague clears it without being asked.” Months later, “when another person brings a similar case, she moves her bag from the spare chair.”

No Meadows ladder has a rung for a chair. Yet here is knowledge passing laterally, without certificate or programme, from one body to another — the rhizome as apprenticeship. The Coda closes the loop: “The guidance now contains something learnt in the conversation that took place there.” The weed has grown through the concrete into the procedure itself.

Chaordic bearing. Movement Three gives the navigator her principles. Weber’s office is order that protects the weak from favour; Arendt’s plurality and Scott’s métis are the generative disorder through which knowledge enters. The Crucible’s resolution — a staged extension, scheduled advice windows, paid learning time, a temporary adjustment to the performance measure — is chaordic design in Hock’s full sense: purpose and principle first, then concept, then structure, then practice. And it is the newer colleague, not the sponsor, who supplies the decisive principle: an authority that leaves her “responsible for deciding beyond her knowledge” is not freedom but exposure.

Movement Four · Understanding and Acting Together — the word above the line

Raymond Tallis grounds the Movement in the mystery of human intelligibility; Weick’s seven properties of sensemaking, McGilchrist’s divergent modes of attention (with an explicit refusal to diagnose institutions as hemispheres), and Roger Miles’s behavioural lens on cognitive load give it method; Bourdieu’s symbolic power and Foucault’s normalising examination show how some accounts are heard as conclusions and others as attitudes. “He calls it common sense. She asks which operating conditions were compared. The meeting hears a conclusion from him and an attitude from her.”

This Movement teaches the hardest rhizomatic lesson: lines of flight do not only liberate; they can stampede. An additional inspection check spreads from one site to two through conversation, sympathy and fear of the last failure; by the time it reaches the forum it has acquired a practical reach exceeding its formal grounds. In the Interlude, a supervisor stands before a planning board on which someone has written required beside the new check. No one can remember when the word appeared. An expressive element has deterritorialised faster than authority could follow, and has already reterritorialised schedules and customer promises around itself (Signal 21: “Authority arrives after expectation”). Rhizomes do not ask permission. That is their genius and their danger, and Donald holds both in view.

Chaordic bearing. Movement Four shows what happens when no one is at the helm of an interpretation. The additional check drifted across sites on the current of “common sense,” and the forum found itself steering after the ship had already turned. Donald’s correction is precise navigation: separate three proposals that had been presented as one; authorise the bounded use the evidence supports; commission the comparison of operating conditions; keep existing requirements in force; return on Friday. He does not reverse the drift by command. He takes a new fix and sets a provisional course.

Movement Five · What Nature Can Teach Us — the discipline of the analogy

Benyus’s biomimicry, Bateson’s ecology of mind, Sheldrake’s fungal worlds, Prigogine’s dissipative structures, Holland, Arthur and Kauffman on adaptation and self-organisation, Maturana and Varela’s autopoiesis, Ashby’s requisite variety, Elmqvist’s response diversity, panarchy, Paul Taylor’s biocentric ethics, biophilic design and Raworth’s doughnut: this is a Movement of extraordinary range, and also of extraordinary restraint. Its governing sentence is a warning: “An analogy needs an explanation.” A company does not acquire biological properties because its members interact; disruption does not become beneficial because some physical systems find new order; a fungal network is not a model of just governance.

I must take that warning personally. The rhizome is itself a botanical analogy — ginger, couch-grass, the iris’s creeping root — and Deleuze and Guattari knew it, which is why they insisted it was a mode of connection, not a plant to be imitated. Donald’s discipline is the condition on which I may borrow their image at all: the rhizome here is a provocation for inquiry, not a law of organisations. And his Crucible shows why. A supplier’s diagram likens a new component to a natural form; the resemblance is made to carry claims about durability, material saving and ecological benefit that each require their own evidence (Signal 23: “Inspiration is carrying the conclusion.”). Against the seduction, the organisation keeps one removed assembly on a shelf, at the cost of a returns credit. Preserving the possibility of another course — the title of one of the Movement’s sections — is the rhizomatic imperative stated as stewardship: keep the line open, even when keeping it open costs something.

Chaordic bearing. Movement Five warns the chaordic navigator against her favourite seduction: the natural metaphor. The chaord itself was born at the Santa Fe Institute among ecologists and physicists, and its literature often treats living systems as warrant. Donald’s discipline — “an analogy needs an explanation” — keeps the compass honest. The decision in the Crucible is a model tack: decline the full replacement order, buy enough to protect immediate service, retain one assembly, accept the lost credit, and record the cost. Neither the order of the supplier’s discount nor the chaos of indefinite delay.

Movement Six · Informal Coalitions — the Thursday call

Chris Rodgers’s informal coalitions, the oldest companion of this anthology, are here deepened through Barnard and Follett on cooperation, Pfeffer and Salancik on resource dependence, the network sciences of Wasserman and Faust, Granovetter, Burt and Borgatti, Iris Marion Young on inclusion and structural injustice, and Bovens and O’Neill on accountability. If any Movement is the rhizome laid bare, it is this one. A colleague offers twenty minutes on Thursday. By Wednesday afternoon three sites are on the invitation; a shared list holds nine entries; two specialist referrals have been held back; and the next morning the invitation has been copied into a planning note as “Weekly cross-site technical support.” No one has agreed to provide it. People have begun arranging work around it.

Here too the network analysts are put on trial, and I find in Donald’s treatment the clearest gloss I know on Deleuze and Guattari’s injunction: make a map, not a tracing. A tracing reproduces what it already presumes — central nodes, prominent brokers — and then hands the picture to management as a ranking of value. A map is “open and connectable,” an experiment in contact with the real. Donald’s planner draws a network and immediately finds that it cannot distinguish someone giving information from someone agreeing to work; the manager admires the lines passing through her, and she asks whether the drawing can also show the time required to maintain those connections. Mapping, Donald insists with Borgatti and Molina, is itself an intervention. The best map is the one examined with those who inhabit it.

Chaordic bearing. Movement Six is where the chaordic path is most vividly drawn and most instructively failed. The Thursday call begins in the generative zone — a modest, bounded gathering around a need — and in forty-eight hours it has slid towards chamos: an invitation become a service, a list become an obligation, referrals held back in hope. The manager’s response is the navigator’s recovery: authorise the call for its original purpose, return separate cases to the established route, remove the recurring invitation, and commit to examining whether a continuing discussion deserves provision. The emergent possibility is kept; the drift is arrested.

Movement Seven · Symbiosis and Renewal — the price of knowing more

Lynn Margulis opens the conversation, and Donald immediately disciplines her: the language of symbiosis “should not settle whether a relationship is beneficial.” Elias’s figurations, Emerson’s power-dependence, Gouldner’s norm of reciprocity, Sen’s capabilities, Hutchins’s distributed cognition, Star and Griesemer’s boundary objects, Carlile’s knowledge boundaries, Orlikowski, Bowker and Star, Arthur, Wiener, Dewey’s ends-in-view, Tronto’s ethics of care and Ostrom’s commons are woven into the most searching account I have read of what organisations owe the relationships that make them capable. This is the territory of Donald’s own Symbiotic Transformation, and it is matured here into a ledger rather than a hymn.

The Crucible contains the volume’s sharpest paradox. Technical support from the supplier is bundled into an annual agreement conditional on a minimum purchase volume — the very volume the review might show to be unnecessary (Signal 35). To fund the knowledge independently, the manager postpones stock replenishment; one assembly remains unreserved and two requests arrive. And in the Interlude, the technician opens the record of an assembly still in service — whose history is thin precisely because nothing went wrong enough to trigger the reporting sequence. This is the rhizome’s secret: the most important line is often the one that never surfaced. Knowledge, Donald shows, is an assemblage too — and assembling it displaces something, which must be named beside the findings.

Chaordic bearing. Movement Seven tests Hock’s own design against Donald’s ledger. A supplier relationship is the purest chaordic opportunity in the volume — two organisations co-producing knowledge neither could make alone — and the commercial terms threaten to reterritorialise it at once, binding the inquiry to the very purchase volume it might question. The manager steers by principle rather than price: fund the review separately, so that what is learned is not owned in advance by what is sold. That is an institutional immune system in miniature. Its cost — a delayed appointment — is entered in the same account.

Movement Eight · Culture and the Social Ecology of Organising — the protected hour

Geertz’s thick description, Schein, Berger and Luckmann on institutionalisation, Gillian Tett’s anthropology of the familiar, Lave and Wenger’s legitimate peripheral participation, Elias and Scotson’s established and outsiders, Stuart Hall on identity, Earley and Ang on cultural intelligence, Lefebvre’s production of space, Edward Hall’s proxemics, Merleau-Ponty’s embodied perception, Suchman’s situated action, Dewey, Wenger, Bateson’s logical levels of learning, Patricia Shaw’s conversational change, and Castoriadis on the imaginary institution of society: the final Movement descends into the lived texture of organisational life and returns with the Invisible Architecture made explicit.

Its Crucible gives an experienced technician one protected hour a week to develop colleagues’ judgement. The appointments are cleared. But the requests that always reached her were never appointments; they come through the doorway anyway. A colleague kindly offers to finish later. The manager takes the waiting request himself — “That it has come to me” — and the hour continues. Then the volume’s last spoken line, which I think is its finest: “I thought keeping the service moving would come first,” says the colleague. “That is what I thought this hour was meant to help us do.”

Here is becoming-other. Not a restructuring: a shift in what an hour means, which is a shift in what expertise is — from the one who answers to the one who makes others able to answer. The Coda records it simply: “A distinction previously carried through her experience is becoming available through another person’s judgement.” Castoriadis would call it an act of instituting. Deleuze would call it a line of flight. Donald calls it reconstitution, and adds, characteristically, that “the organisation has further work to do if that opportunity is to become dependable.”

Chaordic bearing. Movement Eight returns chaordic navigation to its human scale. Hock said that leadership begins with the management of self; the experienced technician’s protected hour is exactly that — a navigator who must reset her own bearing, from being the one who answers to being the one who teaches others to answer. The planner holds order (the cleared schedule); the doorway brings chaos (the requests that were never appointments); the manager steers between by taking the waiting request into his own hands and letting the hour proceed. The Invisible Architecture’s eight orientations — Meaning, Order, Imagination, Governance, Memory, Transformation, Participation, Continuance — read here as a compass rose. Order and Imagination stand opposite one another; Governance and Participation; Memory and Transformation; Meaning and Continuance. Chaordic navigation is the art of holding all eight points in view while choosing a heading.

VII

Deterritorialisation with a Conscience

The rhizomatic image of leverage ends, in its most popular telling, with a weed growing through concrete: you do not strike the slab with a hammer; you find the microscopic fissures and let the root do its slow work until the landscape is shattered and reconfigured from the inside out. It is a magnificent image, and Volume Four is full of weeds — the call before the question, the five words in the handover note, the spare chair, the label on the shelf, the protected hour. But Donald asks the question the image forgets: whose house does the concrete hold up?

A fissure is not neutral. Every line of flight in this volume is paid for by someone: the technical colleague who stopped answering after six; the specialist whose own checking “has delayed another part of her work”; the supervisor at the second site whose learning session is quietly postponed to make another site’s repair trial fit; the customer telephoned about a delay. When the rhizome spreads unexamined, its runners pass through exactly those people with the least room to refuse. Donald’s recurrent questions — who carries the cost of improvement? whose capacity is being spent? can they say the arrangement is becoming untenable without placing themselves at risk? — are the ethics that rhizomatics lacks.

In fairness, Deleuze and Guattari were not naïve about this. They warned that lines of flight can turn into lines of abolition and destruction; that micro-fascisms grow in the same soil as liberations; that “staying stratified — organised, signified, subjected — is not the worst that can happen,” and that the worst is to throw the strata into demented or suicidal collapse. Their counsel was to lodge oneself on a stratum, experiment with what it offers, find the potential movements of deterritorialisation, and proceed with meticulous care. Donald gives that counsel institutional form. Naomi Klein’s Shock Doctrine, which he admits to the conversation in Movement Two, is the case study in deterritorialisation captured by power: crisis cracks the concrete, and those who hold the resources pour the new foundation before anyone else can speak. “A crisis changes the conditions of judgement; it does not discharge the responsibility to give reasons for the order that follows.”

This is why I read Donald’s phrase governance as groundwork — introduced, almost in passing, in Movement Three — as the hinge of the entire volume. Governance is not the hand on the dial. Nor is it the anarchist’s faith that the weed will sort it out. It is the tending of the ground in which fissures open: providing time, cover, routes, records and return dates so that a line of flight can be examined while it is still alive; reinforcing conditions where ambiguity leaves someone exposed; loosening them where an inherited boundary prevents judgement; withdrawing them where a requirement “now consumes attention without supplying a corresponding protection.” The responsible organisation is a gardener of fissures.

Chaordic navigation supplies the gardener’s seamanship. The chaordic path names the two ditches; Donald shows that each is reached by a characteristic misuse of leverage. The control ditch is reached by pulling every lever from outside — more measures, more approvals, more guidance — until “more structure can create less usable understanding.” The chaos ditch is reached by romanticising every fissure — letting arrangements spread on goodwill until an invitation becomes an unprovided service and a helpful colleague becomes the route. Between them, leverage is used as a helmsman uses the rudder: small, continual, corrective movements, each answerable to a bearing that everyone can see. Hock called that bearing purpose and principle. Donald insists that it must also include the conditions under which those who row can continue to row.

THE WEED IS NOT INNOCENT.NEITHER IS THE CONCRETE.

Transformative teleology as the theory of the rhizome

One further convergence deserves to be named. The anthology has travelled across its volumes through three teleologies: formative (the form unfolding what was already implicit), rational (the planner choosing an end and arranging the means) and, now in the ascendant, transformative — the account in which participants “form and are formed” through interaction, so that purposes themselves change. Read beside A Thousand Plateaus, transformative teleology is the rhizome given a social psychology. The tree is formative teleology: everything grows from the seed. The engineer’s blueprint is rational teleology: everything follows the plan. The rhizome is transformative teleology: everything happens in the middle, and the middle changes what the ends are.

Again and again the Crucibles enact it. A repair trial becomes an inquiry into “what maintenance is for.” A question about a component becomes a question about the relationship among operating knowledge, supplier expertise and local learning. A demand for faster checks becomes a question of “what assurance the organisation can responsibly offer.” And then — always — the conscience: “Transformative teleology explains something about how novelty and purpose can emerge through interaction. It cannot by itself establish the ethical adequacy of what emerges.” That sentence, from Movement Seven, should be carved above the door of every organisational-change practice in the land.

VIII

Twelve Theses on Rhizomatic Leverage

Meadows gave us twelve places to intervene in a system. In homage — and in answer — here are twelve theses for intervening within an assemblage, each drawn from the evidence of this volume.

I

LEVERAGE IS IMMANENT.

It is found in the middle of relations, not at a vantage point above them; the manager who believes she stands outside the arrangement is already one of its strings.

II

THE SMALLEST CRACK CARRIES THE WHOLE LOAD.

A telephone call, a chair, five words beneath a note: the fissure is microscopic; its vibration is total.

III

EXPRESSION REORGANISES MATTER.

“Time to learn and check” reallocated cover; “required” reorganised a schedule; a category in a portal can settle what no inquiry has established.

IV

EVERY LINE OF FLIGHT IS RECAPTURED UNLESS IT IS PROVISIONED.

Without time, cover and a destination, the opening becomes a new dependency — “others have begun treating her as the route.”

V

THE MEASURE IS A TRACING; THE CASE IS A MAP.

Follow a few cases through their whole passage, with those who handled them, before the number is allowed to become the story.

VI

DO NOT TRUST THE CENTRE OF THE DIAGRAM.

Centrality may mean value, excess demand or a dependence awaiting support; the line passing through the planner also passes through her evenings.

VII

THE UNSURFACED LINE MATTERS MOST.

The assembly still in service, the request that was never an appointment, the work after six: these are the roots no report has dug up.

VIII

EMERGENCE DOES NOT CONFER LEGITIMACY.

A practice that has spread has proved only that it spreads; authority must still ask whether it should.

IX

CRISIS IS DETERRITORIALISATION — WATCH WHO POURS THE NEW CONCRETE.

Temporary powers, urgent procurements and emergency allocations must carry their reasons into the order that follows.

X

KEEP ANOTHER COURSE ALIVE.

Retain the assembly on the shelf; preserve the alternative while it is still poorly understood rather than proven unsuitable.

XI

BECOMING-OTHER IS A CHANGE IN WHAT THINGS MEAN.

An hour that “keeps the service moving” by teaching rather than answering has changed what expertise is.

XII

GOVERN THE GROUND, NOT THE DIAL.

Governance as groundwork: tend the soil in which fissures open, and remain answerable for what grows.

And three bearings for the chaordic navigator

Twelve theses locate the fissures. Three bearings tell us how to steer once we are in them.

A

STEER BY PURPOSE AND PRINCIPLE, NOT BY RULE AND SET-POINT.

Simple, clear purpose evokes intelligent behaviour — provided the purpose is held open to the transformation that participation brings, and judged by principles that those affected can question.

B

TACK, DO NOT LURCH.

Bounded trials, explicit boundaries of discretion, statements of what is in force now and honest return dates are the small rudder-movements by which an organisation stays on the narrow path between control and chamos.

C

CARRY A LEDGER WITH THE COMPASS.

Every course is rowed by someone. Provision and answerability are the institutional immune system without which every chaord is recaptured — by bureaucracy, by capital, or by the quiet exhaustion of those who kept it moving.

IX

An Invitation, Rewritten

In Sentient Horizon I closed with two sentences: “Our tools may detect patterns; our role is to discern meaning. And, as ever in complex systems, the loop begins again.” I would now amend both. Discernment is not enough; Donald has shown that meaning must be provided for — given time, cover, a route and a reply — or it remains private feeling in a public organisation. And the loop does not simply begin again. A loop returns to itself. A rhizome sends out a runner. Every Coda in this volume ends not with closure but with a runner reaching into the next situation: a customer still waiting, a request already arriving, a sentence someone will one day begin again with “It works provided…”.

That is the deepest achievement of this book. It refuses the false comfort of the finished case. It practises what it describes: an inquiry that stays in the middle, among arrangements already being sustained and altered, with people trying to give an account of what they are doing together and what it asks of them. It is written with a stillness that some readers will mistake for caution. They will be wrong. Its stillness is that of the root in winter — gathering, connecting, preparing the ground for what the formal organisation will only later recognise as change.

Read it as an assemblage. Enter it anywhere. Follow its runners laterally, from a Margin to a Signal to a Lineage to a Crucible and back. Carry its questions into your own organisation and look, first, beneath the floor. You will find there the spare chairs, the unfinished sentences, the shelves and the protected hours of your own institution — the fissures through which it is already becoming otherwise. Volume Five, Donald promises, will take up continuance: what people sustain, revise, relinquish and leave available to those who follow. This volume prepares the soil for it.

Read it, too, as a book of seamanship. Its Signals are soundings; its Orientations are bearings; its Crucibles are passages through narrow water in poor visibility, written by someone who has stood at the helm and knows that the chart is always incomplete. Dee Hock gave us the word for the edge between chaos and order. Donald Davies shows us how to keep a ship there — not by the grandeur of vision, but by the patient, answerable discipline of the next small correction, made in the open, with the crew’s hands and hours counted in.
